# Non-combat challenges: social, exploration, puzzle (cited checklist)

Challenges and triumphs are not only fights. A session that is all combat leaves the social and
exploration players behind. Design these scenes with the same structure you give a fight. Claims
are attributed; items marked unverified came from secondary summaries.

## The shared structure (applies to every non-combat scene)

- **A dramatic question.** State the scene's stake as a yes or no the table is trying to answer
  ("Does the baron lend us troops?", "Do we cross the pass before the storm?"). The scene ends
  when the question is answered, not when a roll is made. (The Angry GM, "Four Things".)
- **A conflict.** Someone or something wants what clashes with the party's goal. No conflict, no
  scene: just narrate it and move on.
- **Decision points.** The players should face real choices, not a single gate.

The 2024 DMG treats social, exploration, and combat as three encounter types to plan, and notes
that objectives need not be combat (its example is infiltrate and disrupt a ritual). This is
reported via D&D Beyond and Sly Flourish summaries, not confirmed against the book.

## Multiple solutions, no single-roll resolution

- Describe the situation first, then adjudicate what the players actually try. Do not hand them a
  menu of correct skills. (The Angry GM objects to menu-style skill challenges: "if the players
  roll the right skills, they win. Or lose. Whatever.")
- Track a named, changing world state rather than one roll: an NPC's patience, a guard's
  alertness, a crowd's mood, a progress clock. The fiction moves it. If a player finds a
  legitimate way to resolve the question, let them win without a roll. (The Angry GM's "whatever
  stat" idea.)
- For each scene, list at least three viable approaches across the party's levers: talk, sneak,
  lore, magic, muscle, craft. Make each one actually work, so different builds shine.

## Clocks and escalating tension

- Use a progress clock, which you can draw live. A danger clock fills toward a bad outcome; a
  racing clock pits the party against a rival clock.
- Tie failed checks to clock progress; a clock can run backward on a strong success. (Attributed
  to the Alexandrian via summary, unverified.) These in-scene danger and racing clocks are
  governed here; a qualifying success is a check that succeeds against the danger.
- Between-session faction clocks are separate: their reduction rule lives in
  `advancing-the-world`, under Ticking a clock down. Both follow Blades in the Dark clocks
  (https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Rules:Progress%20clocks).
- Make the stakes concrete: name what is lost and why the party cannot simply wait.

## Meaningful failure (fail forward)

- A failed roll changes the situation (a clock ticks, a cost is paid, an attitude hardens). It
  does not produce a dead end that stalls the session. (Paizo's Pathfinder 2e GM guidance defines
  fail forward this way; the principle is edition-agnostic.)
- Pre-plan the partial-success and failure outcomes so the story never stops at a locked door or
  a blown Persuasion check.

## By scene type

- **Social.** Know the NPC's want, their line (what they will not do), and what moves them.
  Attitude is a state the party shifts, not a DC they beat once.
- **Exploration and travel.** Make hazards (weather, terrain, hostile wildlife) give clues, so
  careful play and good questions beat raw dice. Reward scouting and preparation.
- **Puzzle.** Give at least two solution paths and a way to earn hints, so the puzzle is a scene
  the party solves, not a wall the session dies on. Let knowledge skills surface clues.

## Spotlight

Cross-check every non-combat scene against the party's spotlight checklist (from
party-composition). Every player should get at least one scene built for their character.
