# Story model: how much story to plan ahead

A cited survey of where the craft literature lands on the railroad-to-sandbox spectrum, and how
the middle-ground tools guard against the two failure modes: too open feels aimless, too
railroaded is not malleable. Claims are attributed. Where a source could not be verified against
its primary text, that is marked.

## The spectrum

**Railroad end.** The Alexandrian calls this the "plotted approach": a start, a chain of scenes,
and an ending. He argues it hands control to the scenario's designer and takes it from the
players, and defines railroading as the GM negating a player's choice to enforce a preconceived
outcome. (That definition appears in his reply to a comment on "Don't Prep Plots", not in the
essay body.)

**Structured middle**, from more structure to less:

- Sly Flourish (Lazy DM): the prep list includes loose scene ideas ("Outline Potential Scenes")
  and about ten secrets and clues, which are facts, not a script. It also carries a strong start
  and up to three Fronts. He describes a Front as a sinister campaign moving in parallel to the
  PCs.
- The Alexandrian: prep situations, goal-driven opponents, and toolkits, not contingencies.
  Node-based design (a web of locations, people, or events linked by clues) keeps the scenario
  open.
- Dungeon World: Fronts hold dangers, each with grim portents (the steps it takes) and an
  impending doom (where it ends if unopposed).
- Blades in the Dark: progress clocks track a goal, and faction clocks advance during downtime.

**Emergent end.** Apocalypse World's MC agenda includes "play to find out what happens." Dungeon
World and Blades both sit near this end but add structure: the world's own threats keep moving
on clocks.

**Colville.** Matt Colville's Running the Game is widely credited with villain guidance (villains
act, put heroes on the defensive, and must be beatable), but this could not be verified against
the primary videos. Treat as unverified until you watch them.

## Where they converge

- Prep the opposition and the world, not the outcome. Ask what the villain wants, not what the
  players will do. (Alexander, Shea.)
- The threat moves on its own clock, whether or not the PCs engage. (Shea's Front text; Blades
  faction clocks.) Player choices change whether it is stopped, redirected, or succeeds.
- The path to any needed conclusion is redundant. The Three Clue Rule: at least three clues per
  conclusion. Shea's secrets are written as standalone facts, decoupled from where they are
  found. (Alexander, Shea.)
- Size the doom sensibly. Shea warns against a world-ending result that leaves players no choice
  but to engage.
- Keep prep light. Draw maps and leave blanks. (Shea.)

## Recommended default: a one-shot that grows into a campaign

1. A strong start: open in the middle of action with a clear immediate problem.
2. One villain, written as a goal plus three to five portents and a result. Size the result so
   it cannot force the players' hand.
3. Three to five nodes (a person, place, or event each), linked by about ten secrets and clues,
   each reachable at least three ways.
4. One or two clocks: a 6-segment clock for the villain's plan, optionally a 4-segment clock for
   a local pressure.
5. Advance a portent after the session, not mid-scene. Tick the clock on a consequence or
   elapsed time. Between sessions, ask what the villain did.
6. At the end of the one-shot, keep the best surprise: promote one unresolved portent, NPC, or
   location into the next session's strong start. Add a second Front only when the first feels
   settled, up to three.
7. Do not write scenes. Prep the situation and improvise the scene.

## How each tool prevents each failure mode

Aimless (too open):

- The strong start supplies an opening hook.
- The villain's goal and portents give a direction that does not wait on the players choosing
  one.
- Visible clock ticks make pressure felt; the impending result supplies stakes.
- Secrets and clues tell players where to go next.

Railroaded (not malleable):

- A situation has no fixed sequence, so players cannot fail a step in a chain. (Alexander calls
  plots "a chain of potential points of failure".)
- Nodes let the party enter any way, find another way, or skip a part.
- The Three Clue Rule means a missed clue does not force you to steer them back.
- Because the Front advances regardless of engagement, player choices change the outcome rather
  than being negated.
