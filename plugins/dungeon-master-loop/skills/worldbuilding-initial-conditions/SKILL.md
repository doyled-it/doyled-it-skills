# Worldbuilding initial conditions

## Overview

Set the initial conditions, then let play run. A world you can improvise in is not a world you
have fully written. It is a small set of starting conditions (a place, a pressure, a cast, a
few unresolved threads) arranged so that each session's events follow from them. You prep the
situation, not the plot. This is the "physics engine" idea: define the forces, and the story is
what happens when the players push on them.

This skill produces the world artifacts the rest of the loop reads: setting canon, the Front,
faction clocks, secrets and clues, and hooks and seeds. It runs in two passes, gated around
session zero (see `dungeon-master-loop`).

## How much story to plan: situations, not plots

The craft literature converges on a middle ground between a railroad (a fixed chain of scenes)
and a formless sandbox. Read `references/story-model.md` for the cited survey and the two
failure modes it guards against. The short version:

- Prep the world and the opposition, not the outcome. Ask what the villain is trying to do, not
  what the players will do about it.
- The central threat advances on its own clock whether or not the players engage. Their choices
  change whether it is stopped, redirected, or succeeds.
- Make the path to any needed conclusion redundant (at least three clues per conclusion), so a
  missed clue never forces you to steer.
- Keep the stakes sized so the players are never left with no real choice but to engage.

This prevents both failure modes. Aimless is cured by a visible pressure that moves and tells
the players where the heat is. Railroaded is cured by having no scripted sequence to fail out
of: situations survive any order of play.

## Pass one: the premise (before session zero)

Produce only enough to pitch the game and to let players build characters that belong in it.

1. **The world in one paragraph.** Tone, genre dial, the one thing that makes this place this
   place. No gazetteer.
2. **The landing region.** One home settlement plus one nearby adventure site. Name both.
   Nothing more yet.
3. **The Front (the central pressure).** The single external force squeezing this region: a
   rising cult, a failing border, a buried power waking. Write it as the villain's goal, three
   to five portents (the steps the plan takes if unopposed), and an impending result. Size the
   result so it does not force the players' hand. The Front is the campaign's slow burn.

Gate (Phase 1 in the loop): a one-paragraph premise and a stated central pressure exist.

Stop here. Session zero and party-composition run next. You need the players' bonds and wants
before you finalize the cast and where they land.

## Pass two: finalize (after session zero and party-composition)

Now you know the characters, and session zero has produced the table safety artifact. Read the
lines and veils before you generate the cast and villains: a villain's methods, an NPC's history,
and the Front's threat must not cross a line, and veiled content stays off screen. Then tie the
world to the characters.

4. **Factions.** Two or three. For each, write only members the party can meet, territory they
   can visit, and a goal that conflicts with another faction. Give each a clock (see
   `advancing-the-world` for clock sizes and how clocks tick). A faction without a playable
   surface is lore, not a faction.
5. **The cast.** A short roster of major NPCs and villains (named, with a want and a lever) and
   a list of minor NPCs (name and one line only, fleshed out on demand). Tie at least one
   villain or NPC to a player character's bond or want. That tie is what makes the opening
   personal.
6. **A thin pantheon.** Two or three gods maximum: one patron, one antagonist cult, optionally
   one neutral. One symbol and one desire each. Leave their true nature unanswered on purpose.
7. **The landing position.** Exactly how and where play opens, pointed at a player's want. This
   becomes the strong start that session-prep builds on.
8. **Secrets and the iceberg.** Write about ten short true facts the players could learn, at
   least three pointing at the Front or an unbuilt place (these are campaign seeds). Name two or
   three people or places the players will hear about but not visit yet, and do not build them.
   Park those in hooks and seeds.

Gate (Phase 4 in the loop): landing position set, at least one villain or NPC tied to a player
character, factions have clocks, roughly ten secrets exist.

## Outputs (artifacts handed forward)

In Obsidian (the default binding), combat-capable villains and major NPCs are written as
bestiary-indexed statblocks (an NPC note that is also a `statblock: inline` creature), per
`../dungeon-master-loop/references/obsidian-ttrpg-formats.md`, so they are ready to fight the
moment play reaches them.

- Setting canon: the region, settlements, factions, pantheon.
- The Front: goal, portents, result.
- Faction clocks: one per faction (sizes are defined in `advancing-the-world`: 4 for a fast
  scheme, 6 or 8 for a slow burn, 12 for a long arc).
- Secrets and clues: about ten, some pointing past what is built.
- Hooks and seeds: the named-but-unbuilt iceberg.

session-prep reads all of these. advancing-the-world writes back to all of these after play.

## The one rule

Never fully resolve everything, and never build ahead of the players. Build the ten percent the
table will touch, imply the ninety percent through named-but-unseen places, unresolved factions,
and clues that point outward. Unfinished clocks and unfound secrets are what pull the group into
the next session.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a plot (a sequence of planned scenes) instead of a situation (forces and a cast).
- Building factions, gods, or regions the party cannot reach this arc. That is lore, and it is
  the second pass's enemy.
- Finalizing the cast before session zero, so no villain is tied to a real character.
- A Front with a doom so total the players have no choice. Keep the stakes answerable.
